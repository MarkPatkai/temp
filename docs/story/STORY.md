# The Crossroads

Worldbuilding, narrative foundation, characters, and the beginning of the player's journey

"A thousand kingdoms. A hundred gods. Countless wars. And somewhere between them all, a place where a stranger can still become someone."

I would build your story around one central idea: The Crossroads is a place where everyone can belong, but nobody is truly free from the world they came from.

Your concept has the foundations for a very interesting open-ended fantasy RPG. Rather than making the player a chosen hero tasked with saving the world, we can make them an ordinary individual trying to establish a life, discover their strengths, and decide what kind of person they want to become.

The world will have politics, wars, monsters, magic, ancient civilizations, and competing ideologies. However, none of these should automatically become the player's responsibility.

The player can be a merchant, adventurer, craftsman, mercenary, scholar, explorer, or even eventually a political figure. The story supplies opportunities and consequences rather than prescribing one heroic destiny.

## 1. The world and its history

The known world is called Elyndra (working name).

Elyndra has many races, civilizations, religions, and magical traditions. Some kingdoms have existed for thousands of years, while others emerged from recent wars or revolutions.

Different races have their ancestral homelands, but very few civilizations remain exclusively inhabited by a single race.

A human kingdom might have a large dwarven merchant population. An elven city might have generations of half-elven inhabitants who have never visited either of their ancestral homelands.

Most importantly, race does not determine political allegiance, personality, or morality.

An elf from a militant empire might have considerably more in common with a human soldier than with an elf raised in a peaceful woodland settlement.

### The major races

These are examples of the primary races and their subcategories. Each primary race has at least one major homeland, while its sub-races may have smaller settlements, enclaves, or communities scattered throughout the world.

| Main race | Sub-races                                    | Cultural and political foundations                                                      |
| --------- | -------------------------------------------- | --------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Humans    | Highlanders, Lowlanders, Easterners          | Numerous kingdoms, republics, and empires, often competing with each other              |
| Elves     | High Elves, Wood Elves, Dark Elves           | Ancient homeland with many independent enclaves and different magical traditions        |
| Dwarves   | Mountain Dwarves, Deep Dwarves, Hill Dwarves | Great subterranean holds, industrial cities, and merchant communities                   |
| Orcs      | Plains Orcs, Iron Orcs, Ash Orcs             | Tribal confederations, fortified cities, and militarized kingdoms                       |
| Beastkin  | Catfolk, Wolfkin, Foxfolk                    | A broad family of peoples with different settlements, customs, and political identities |
| Drakari   | Emberborn, Stoneborn, Stormborn              | Draconic peoples with ancestral strongholds and powerful magical traditions             |

Half-races are also recognized throughout Elyndra. Half-elves and half-orcs might be common in border regions, while other combinations are rare or depend on magical and biological compatibility.

Some half-races have developed their own identities and communities over generations. Others identify primarily with one of their parent cultures.

This gives you a useful design distinction: ancestry determines certain inherited characteristics, but upbringing determines much of the character's cultural knowledge, education, and starting opportunities.

Its official name is The Free Concord of Crossroads, although most people simply call the entire country Crossroads.

Its capital is Veybridge, a sprawling city at the confluence of major rivers and historic trade routes.

The country controls a fertile central valley, a collection of mountain passes, several trade towns, and the roads connecting the surrounding neutral states.

Crossroads was not founded by a single race.

Centuries ago, following a devastating period of conflict known as the War of Seven Banners, merchants, refugees, displaced nobles, and representatives of several civilizations established a settlement where people could trade without surrendering their allegiance to a foreign ruler.

Over time, this settlement became a sovereign country.

Its founding agreement, the Concord of Open Roads, established three important principles:

1. No individual shall be denied lawful residence, trade, or protection solely because of race or birthplace.
2. The country shall not participate in foreign wars unless its sovereignty or inhabitants are directly threatened.
3. Foreign nobility and governments possess no sovereign authority within Crossroads, regardless of their historical claims or influence.

These principles are admired, criticized, and frequently tested.

### Neutrality is difficult

Crossroads has no desire to become a military empire, but neutrality requires considerable diplomatic and economic power.

It is protected by trade agreements, defensive fortifications, and the fact that almost every major country benefits from its continued independence.

However, there is an increasing problem.

Crossroads is growing.

Its population is expanding faster than its settlements can accommodate. Trade routes require protection. Monsters and bandits threaten the surrounding countryside, and small neighboring countries have begun requesting assistance from Crossroads.

The government has begun to worry that remaining passive will eventually weaken its position.

This is where the Adventurers' Guild enters the story.

## 3. The Adventurers' Guild and the new initiative

The Adventurers' Guild is an independent organization with branches across Elyndra.

Although many people associate it with monster hunting, its responsibilities are far broader: escorting merchants, searching for missing people, mapping dangerous regions, recovering artifacts, resolving local emergencies, and completing contracts that ordinary authorities cannot handle efficiently.

The Crossroads branch is among the largest in the world.

Its latest initiative is known as the Open Charter Initiative.

THE OPEN CHARTER INITIATIVE

## A home for those willing to build one.

The government grants eligible applicants a small parcel of land within designated settlement territories. In exchange, recipients agree to register with local authorities, respect existing property and passage rights, and contribute to the region's development.

Applicants are not required to become professional adventurers. However, the Guild facilitates registration, training, contracts, and access to essential services.

The initiative is intended to attract capable individuals who can become the next generation of settlers, tradespeople, explorers, and regional protectors.

The initiative benefits both sides.

The government gains new inhabitants and economic activity. The Guild gains a larger pool of people who might accept contracts. The settlers receive an opportunity they might never have enjoyed in their homelands.

Importantly, the land belongs to the player, subject to the charter's ordinary legal conditions. It isn't a temporary camp lent by the Guild.

The player can develop it into a house, workshop, farm, training ground, trading establishment, or another kind of property.

### The controversy

Not everyone supports the initiative.

Some believe it will bring prosperity and protect the countryside. Others believe the government is using inexperienced foreigners to populate dangerous frontier lands.

There are also concerns that powerful individuals and foreign organizations could exploit the charter to acquire influence inside Crossroads.

Even more importantly, some parcels border existing villages or lands used seasonally by local communities. The government may consider a parcel unoccupied while nearby inhabitants consider it part of their traditional territory.

These tensions are excellent sources of early quests. They create conflicts in which there may be several reasonable solutions rather than a simple good-versus-evil outcome.

## 4. The surrounding political landscape

I particularly like your idea of gradually increasing political and cultural distinction as the player moves farther away from Crossroads.

I'd make that a deliberate worldbuilding principle.

The Inner Concord — Crossroads itself

Multicultural cities, international guilds, diverse neighborhoods, protected roads, and relatively consistent laws. Conflicts are primarily commercial, civic, or personal.

The Neutral Marches — neighboring states

Small principalities, free towns, independent settlements, and loosely governed territories. Crossroads has influence but not sovereignty. Local traditions become more important.

The Outer Kingdoms — established powers

Distinct civilizations with their own institutions, religions, military traditions, and social expectations. Political affiliation begins to matter considerably.

The Distant Realms — isolated and powerful nations

Ancient empires, difficult terrain, unfamiliar magical traditions, contested borders, and countries that rarely receive independent adventurers.

Distance also matters practically. Although Crossroads is the center of international commerce, caravans often take weeks to reach distant countries. Dangerous passes, political borders, seasonal weather, and monster-infested regions complicate travel.

Being a trade hub doesn't mean every destination is readily accessible.

### Two very different human countries

The Varric Empire is a militaristic human empire that prizes discipline, loyalty, territorial expansion, and military achievement. Its citizens are not inherently cruel, but service to the empire is deeply embedded in their culture.

The Republic of Meridia is a wealthy mercantile nation that values contractual law, personal wealth, commercial competition, and political representation. It prefers economic influence over direct conquest.

Both are predominantly human. Both consider themselves civilized and prosperous. Neither particularly likes the other's methods.

A player originating from the Varric Empire might have military training and knowledge of imperial customs. Someone from Meridia might understand negotiation and commerce.

Yet their race provides no meaningful explanation for those differences. Their background does.

This same approach should apply to every major race.

# 5. The main cast

I would give the early game a relatively small cast of recurring characters, each representing a different perspective on life in Crossroads.

Their opinions should evolve depending on the player's decisions, and they should have ambitions that exist independently of the player.

# 6. The opening: A stranger at the gates

Prologue — The First Day

The opening takes place outside Veybridge, in a line of travelers awaiting entry into the capital.

A merchant complains about customs. Two dwarves argue over cargo weight. A young adventurer struggles to explain the legality of carrying a captured slime in a glass container.

The player reaches the registration desk.

This is where character creation begins.

### Scene 1 — Identity and origin

Gate Officer: "Name?"

Player: [Enter name]

Gate Officer: "Right. Race and ancestry?"

The player selects their race, sub-race, or half-race.

Gate Officer: "Place of birth?"

The player chooses their homeland or region of origin.

Gate Officer: "And what brings you to Crossroads? Work? Family? Running away from something?"

Possible responses include:

- "I want to become an adventurer."
- "I'm looking for a place to settle."
- "I want to earn enough money to start over."
- "I have business with the Guild."
- "I'd rather not say."

This is not simply cosmetic. The selection can influence initial dialogue, personal objectives, starting reputation, and available introductory quests.

A player who chose a military background might receive different reactions from a guard than someone who chose a scholarly background.

### Scene 2 — The Guild assessment

The player arrives at the Guild Hall, where Mara Vell greets the new applicants.

Mara: "Welcome to the Adventurers' Guild. If you're here for the Open Charter, you're in the right place."

Lucien: "Excellent! I've been looking forward to this. Where do we receive our assignments?"

Mara: "Assignments?"

Lucien: "Yes. Our first quests. Monster slaying, perhaps?"

Mara: "You haven't even registered."

Lucien: "Ah. Of course. Administrative necessities."

Mara: "You're going to enjoy meeting Borin."

Rather than immediately assigning the player a permanent class, the Guild performs a short assessment.

The player answers questions about previous experience, practical skills, and preferred training.

For example, an applicant might have worked as a soldier, farmer, apprentice mage, traveling merchant, hunter, noble attendant, or laborer.

Their answers determine starting competencies.

A former soldier might begin with increased weapon proficiency and physical conditioning. A scholar might have greater magical knowledge, literacy, and research abilities.

The player then selects an initial combat or professional specialization, but this is a starting point, not a lifetime restriction.

A farmer can become a powerful mage. A novice swordsman can eventually study alchemy. Someone with no magical experience may discover an affinity for magic much later.

### Scene 3 — The land deed

After registration, the player is directed to Borin's office.

Borin: "Application accepted. Identification verified. No outstanding warrants. Good."

Player: "That's it?"

Borin: "No. That's the easy part."

Player: "What's the difficult part?"

Borin: "Understanding what you're receiving."

Borin places a deed on the desk.

Borin: "You are being granted a parcel in the eastern settlement district. The land is yours to develop, provided you comply with the terms of the Charter."

Player: "And what is already there?"

Borin: "An abandoned cottage foundation, a well of uncertain condition, and some exceptionally enthusiastic weeds."

Player: "Anything else?"

Borin: "A road."

Player: "That sounds useful."

Borin: "It would be, if anyone had maintained it in the past thirty years."

He explains that the land can be improved over time and that applicants can undertake Guild contracts to finance construction.

The government will not build a home for the player.

The Guild provides a modest starter allowance, essential equipment, and directions to the parcel.

The rest is up to them.

The player's first property is provisionally named Briarfield.

It lies a few hours from Veybridge, close enough to access Guild services but far enough into the countryside that the player can develop it independently.

There is no elaborate mansion, no magical fortress, and no army of followers waiting to serve them.

Initially, the property has a damaged foundation, a neglected well, a few trees, and enough space for modest construction.

The property serves several purposes.

It is the player's home, a place to store possessions, undertake crafting, recover between journeys, and potentially establish a business or recruit residents.

More importantly, it is a tangible record of the player's decisions.

A dedicated adventurer might construct training facilities and equipment storage.

A merchant might establish a warehouse and trading post.

A mage could create a research laboratory and magical garden.

A player interested in settlement management might gradually expand the estate into a small community.

The land itself should not force the player into a particular profession or playstyle.

# 8. The first quests and emerging storyline

I would resist introducing a world-ending threat during the opening hours. The initial problems should be local, comprehensible, and sometimes entirely mundane.

The first Guild noticeboard can present several contracts.

| Quest                    | Premise                                                          | What it introduces                         |
| ------------------------ | ---------------------------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------ |
| The Missing Shipment     | A merchant wagon failed to reach a nearby village                | Exploration, combat, trade reputation      |
| Weeds and Worse          | Unusual creatures have infested farmland near the settlement     | Gathering, basic combat, local relations   |
| A Disputed Boundary      | Two households disagree about a newly registered property        | Dialogue, law, alternative quest solutions |
| The Apprentice's Mistake | A magical experiment has caused minor disturbances               | Magic, investigation, crafting             |
| The Old Road             | An abandoned route needs to be surveyed before repairs can begin | Exploration, mapping, hidden locations     |

The player can choose which contracts to accept and in what order.

Completing a contract improves their reputation with the relevant people or organizations, but solving a problem through diplomacy may produce a different reputation outcome than resolving it through force.

### The first longer questline: The Old Road

A surveyor assigned to map a road near Briarfield has disappeared.

At first, the Guild assumes a monster attack.

Elira offers to guide the player to the surveyor's last known position, provided the player agrees to help her investigate a separate boundary dispute.

Along the road, the player discovers damaged survey markers, evidence of recent travelers, and a sealed underground passage.

Several explanations emerge.

The surveyor might have encountered smugglers. Local residents may have intentionally interfered with the survey to protect their land claims. Or the road may conceal something the government didn't know existed.

Eventually, the player discovers that an old route crosses land claimed by several different communities.

The missing surveyor had uncovered records that contradicted the government's official maps.

This is where the story becomes interesting.

The player can assist the authorities, support the neighboring settlement's claim, help negotiate shared access, or simply submit the evidence to the Guild and leave the dispute to others.

All are legitimate choices.

The situation also introduces Selene's research into the ancient magical road network, creating a possible path into much larger adventures.

## 9. Reputation: Becoming someone

The player begins as an unknown newcomer.

They aren't immediately trusted by foreign diplomats, admired by nobles, or respected by veteran adventurers.

Reputation is something earned through actions.

I would divide it into three distinct concepts.

Guild standing measures professional achievement. It determines the complexity and prestige of contracts available. A powerful individual isn't necessarily a high-ranked adventurer; Guild rank also reflects reliability, completed assignments, and professional recognition.

Faction reputation measures relationships with governments, organizations, settlements, and institutions. Helping a Varric envoy may improve relations with the empire but concern a rival Mer idian representative. Not every faction conflict needs to be zero-sum.

Personal reputation measures how individuals and smaller communities perceive the player. Someone might be widely respected by the Guild but disliked by a particular village because of an earlier decision.

These distinctions allow for a more personal narrative.

A high-level adventurer might remain politically insignificant. A talented merchant could acquire extraordinary influence without ever defeating a powerful monster. A scholar might become renowned for discoveries rather than combat.

The world recognizes different kinds of achievement.

# 10. The wider story

The Open Charter Initiative should remain the overarching narrative anchor, but I wouldn't write a single main questline that the player is required to follow.

Instead, I would structure the narrative around three overlapping stories.

### The personal story — Who am I becoming?

The player's chosen background establishes where they came from, but not where they must go.

Their starting circumstances may offer a personal objective: restoring a family reputation, earning independence, recovering lost knowledge, establishing a business, or simply surviving somewhere new.

As the player gains experience, they may redefine that objective.

The most important story is the one created by their chosen profession, relationships, home, and adventures.

### The regional story — What will Crossroads become?

The Open Charter Initiative becomes increasingly influential.

New settlements emerge. Trade routes expand. Local communities challenge government decisions. Foreign nations begin sending their own applicants.

Over time, the Guild and government face a fundamental question:

Can Crossroads continue welcoming everyone without eventually being controlled by someone?

There should be several factions with competing answers, not a single secretly evil organization.

Some want stronger government oversight. Others want greater local independence. Merchants prefer fewer restrictions. Frontier residents demand more protection.

The player's influence depends on how involved they choose to become.

### The world story — What lies beyond the roads?

The ancient network discovered beneath Crossroads hints at a time when the world's civilizations were far more connected.

Perhaps the present political geography is not entirely natural.

Ancient roads and transit gates once linked distant regions, and their collapse isolated populations for centuries.

Some nations might want the network restored. Others may have good reasons to keep it buried.

This provides a long-term mystery with implications for exploration, magic, trade, and international politics.

However, it should remain optional until the player actively decides to investigate.

Not every adventurer needs to uncover the world's history.

## 11. What I would preserve as the game's narrative identity

The strongest element of your idea is that the player arrives as a nobody and gradually establishes a place in a living world.

I would preserve that rather than making the character secretly royalty, a reincarnated god, or the only individual capable of averting disaster.

Crossroads offers a home, but the world offers possibilities.

There can be small, wholesome adventures involving missing animals and village festivals, alongside much more serious stories about civil wars, forbidden magic, political assassinations, and competing territorial claims.

The player chooses how deeply to involve themselves.

And the characters should reflect that freedom. Some will become friends, others rivals or enemies. Some may simply be people the player meets once during a journey.

The main story is not about saving Crossroads. It is about finding out what Crossroads means to the player.

An excellent opening line for the game might be:

> "Welcome to Crossroads. Whatever you were before you arrived, you're free to become someone else here."

And a fitting final exchange for the prologue, after the player receives their deed:

Borin: "One last matter."

Player: "More paperwork?"

Borin: "No. A piece of advice."

Player: "I'm listening."

Borin: "You came here to start a new life. Many people do. Just remember that a new life doesn't erase the old one."

Player: "Is that supposed to be encouraging?"

Borin: "I'm a registrar, not a poet."

The player receives the deed to Briarfield.

Prologue completed — A Place to Call Your Own.

New objective: Visit your property.

At this point, the introduction ends and the world opens up.
